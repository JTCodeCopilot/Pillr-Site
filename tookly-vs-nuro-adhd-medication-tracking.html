Comparison

Tookly vs Nuro
for ADHD medication tracking

Tookly and Nuro both help people record ADHD medication on Apple devices. Tookly is built around reminder escalation, explicit dose states, widgets, refills, and optional family activity sharing. Nuro puts medication reminders beside an effect timeline, symptoms, side effects, sleep, daily context, and visual analytics.

Published September 22, 2026 · Last reviewed September 22, 2026

A note from Pillr

This article is published by Pillr. We make a medication tracking app, but this page compares Tookly and Nuro for people deciding between those two options.

Quick answer

Choose Tookly if the main need is a persistent reminder routine with calm, repeating, or supported full-screen alarm options, followed by explicit taken, missed, skipped, and catch-up records.

Choose Nuro if you want medication reminders and history beside an adjustable effect timeline, symptoms, side effects, mood, focus, sleep, activity, daily context, and visual analytics.

Both are currently Apple-focused and free to download with paid features. Tookly is iPhone-focused and does not require a Tookly account. Nuro supports iPhone and iPad and describes account-based cloud syncing and backup.

Who this comparison is for

This comparison is for someone choosing an Apple-focused ADHD medication tracker and deciding between reminder persistence and a broader daily-context record. It is especially useful if dose states, missed-dose review, symptoms, timing estimates, pill supply, family activity sharing, privacy, or device support will decide the choice.

It is not a guide to choosing medication, changing a dose, setting a medical interval, deciding what to do after a missed dose, or treating a pattern or timing estimate as medical proof.

Key differences

Tookly is reminder-escalation led. Its current App Store page lists Gentle, Nagging, and optional Strong Alarm modes, interactive widgets, a reminder Live Activity, explicit dose states, missed-dose Catch Up, stock tracking, and Guardian family sharing.

Nuro is daily-context led. Its current official site and App Store page describe medication reminders and history beside an effect timeline, symptoms, side effects, mood, focus, sleep, activity, daily factors, inventory, and visual analytics.

The practical choice is whether you want the reminder and follow-through to lead the experience, or a wider record of what happened around medication.

Detailed comparison by use case

If reminders are the main problem

Tookly offers three reminder styles. Nagging reminders repeat until the user responds within a chosen limit, while the optional Strong Alarm uses Apple's alarm permission on supported iPhones. Tookly also lists snooze, widgets, a reminder Live Activity, and Focus Filter controls.

Nuro lists custom medication reminders and schedules but gives less public detail about escalation and response controls. Check the live app for the exact repeat, snooze, and notification actions you need.

No app can guarantee every operating-system alert will arrive exactly on time. Test the chosen setup with notification, Focus, sound, battery, and device settings.

If dose states and missed-dose review matter

Tookly explicitly lists taken, missed, and skipped records, a Catch Up checklist, history correction, and a short Undo period. Its calendar, heatmap, and streak views are built around following the dose record over time.

Nuro supports quick dose logging and medication history. Its current public material does not clearly document every missed, skipped, postponed, or as-needed action, so verify the exact states you need before moving an established record.

If timing, symptoms, or daily context matter

Nuro's Effect Timeline uses a medication profile that can be adjusted. It also places symptoms, side effects, mood, focus, sleep, steps, caffeine, meals, water, and visual analytics close to the medication record.

Tookly lists a next-dose countdown but not a comparable effect timeline, symptom journal, or daily-context workflow. It is the more focused reminder-and-history option between these two apps.

A timeline or pattern can help organise questions, but it cannot prove when medication started working or whether medication caused a symptom.

If refills, reports, or family support matter

Tookly lists remaining stock, an estimated run-out date, refill alerts, CSV export, a paid 35-day report, and paid Guardian sharing for recent activity.

Nuro lists inventory tracking and data export, but its current public information does not describe a comparable family activity-sharing workflow. Check the export format, paid boundaries, and inventory correction tools in either app before relying on them.

If privacy, accounts, and backup decide the choice

Tookly does not require an account. Its current privacy policy says medication data is stored locally and may sync through Apple iCloud when the user is signed in. It separately describes Firebase Analytics, Crashlytics, and subscription processing while saying medication names, doses, and health records are not sent to Firebase.

Nuro's current privacy policy describes Apple Sign-In, cloud storage, syncing, backup, and account deletion. Its App Store privacy label separately lists identifiers under tracking and linked data, while the website policy says it does not track users across other apps or websites. Review both current disclosures before storing sensitive information.

Where Tookly fits better

  • You want a choice between calm, repeating, and supported full-screen reminder styles.
  • You want explicit taken, missed, skipped, and Catch Up records.
  • You want widgets, a reminder Live Activity, stock estimates, and refill alerts to lead the routine.
  • You want optional Guardian family sharing or iCloud syncing without creating a Tookly account.

Where Nuro fits better

  • You want symptoms, side effects, mood, focus, sleep, activity, and daily context near the medication record.
  • You want an adjustable effect profile and visual analytics to be central to review.
  • You want iPad support and account-based syncing across supported Apple devices.
  • You want medication inventory and wider pattern review in the same app.

When neither app may be enough

If you need a dedicated appointment-preparation workflow, explicit CSV and PDF medication-history exports, structured post-dose reflections, or an on-device medication record without medication cloud syncing, Pillr is a separate ADHD medication tracking option to consider.

What to verify before choosing

  • Whether the app supports your exact medication, formulation, and prescribed schedule.
  • How taken, missed, skipped, postponed, late, snoozed, and as-needed doses appear in history.
  • Whether the reminder style works with your iPhone's notification, Focus, sound, battery, and alarm settings.
  • How each timing view is adjusted and how clearly it is presented as an estimate.
  • Which reminders, reports, exports, inventory, analytics, and sharing features need paid access.
  • How account creation, iCloud or cloud storage, backup, analytics, tracking disclosures, export, and deletion work.
  • Whether the app supports every phone or tablet you plan to use.

Is Tookly or Nuro better for ADHD medication tracking?

Tookly may fit better if persistent reminder styles, missed-dose review, widgets, refill alerts, and optional family activity sharing matter. Nuro may fit better if symptoms, side effects, sleep, daily context, an effect timeline, and visual analytics matter.

Do Tookly and Nuro both include medication reminders?

Yes. Tookly lists Gentle, Nagging, and supported Strong Alarm options, while Nuro lists custom medication schedules and reminders. Check the current app and device settings for the exact reminder controls you need.

Do Tookly and Nuro require an account?

Tookly says no account is required and describes local storage with optional iCloud syncing. Nuro's privacy information describes Apple Sign-In, an account, and cloud storage for syncing and backup.

Do Tookly or Nuro replace medical advice?

No. They are tracking and reminder tools. Questions about doses, timing, side effects, symptoms, missed medication, or app-generated timing views need advice from a qualified healthcare professional.
